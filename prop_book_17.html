TIP LOSS

17   Tip loss

17.1   The nature of the tip loss

Tip loss differs essentially from the losses we have encountered so far. Consider the ( ring ) thrust equation (7.5), repeated here :

d T = d m • . w (17.1)

or its non-dimensional version (7.12) :

dT  ′ ( x ) = d m  ′ . b ( x ) (17.2)

All of the analyses and scheduling we applied so far to optimize the rotational and viscous local loss ratio and efficiency, acted exclusively on the induction term w, c.q. b . The mass flow dm● c.q. m ′ had no direct influence whatsoever on the local efficiency, only on the thrust.

  Tip loss does not act on w. Instead, it acts on the mass flow term dm●.

  The optimizations (XXXXXXXXXXXXX) and (16.12) for the rotational and viscous loss both act on the distribution of the local induction b(x). They are functions of the local pitch angle alone, and by the geometrical relations (2.4) to (2.8) they only depend on the non-dimensional radius x.

  These optimizations are independent of the mass flow ( at least under the light loading assumption ). For equal momentum efficiency, b(x) is reduced locally.

The loss of efficiency occurs when b(x) is uniformly inflated to restore the original overall thrust.

  Changing dm●, even locally, does not change the optimal distrubution of the induction with blade radius.

  Tip loss does affect the mass which receives that induction. Some of the air in the original momentum tube escapes around the blade tips, and is not accelerated. By (17.1) this does affect the thrust. For a given induction and therefore for a given efficiency, there will be less thrust. To compensate, the induction over the active part of the prop disk will have to increase. By this indirect route, the tip loss reduces the efficiency for a given thrust after all.

17.2   Thrust loss in the spaces between the blades

The previous chapters all gave velocity diagrams at the blade element. This was enough to deduce local efficiencies, and to schedule an optimal shape for the distribution of the local induction.

  In the presence of tip loss, the axial induction velocities in the spaces between the blades will be less than the induced velocity at the blades, but this does not affect the reasoning at the blade elements themselves.

  However, it would seem proper to determine the average induction in a full ring of the flow tube, and find a reduced thrust from this in equation (17.1).

  But it is not a simple matter to find the induction in the spaces between the blades, and we will find that in some way it will not be needed.

  The other option is to find an average, "effective" value for the ring mass flow dm●. Conceptually, the blades still give the local air the full induction b(x), but in Newton's law of (17.1) they do not "feel" the resistance of the full mass flow through the ring pushing back on them.

  By this concept, (17.1) with the full induction b(x) applies for the whole ring, provided we can find the reduced ring mass flow dm●.

  Tip loss theory is built around this concept.

17.3   A local mass flow ratio

We made the local thrust (7.4) non-dimensional in (7.5), by introducing a non-dimensional mass flow dm ′.

  We now define a function K ( x ) for the reduction of the effective local mass flow, due to "tip loss". This changes the light-loading momentum flow from (7.11) into :

d m  ′ ≡ 2  x . d x X 2 . K ( x ) (17.3)

For heavy loading, we can also retain the term ( 1 + ½ b ) from (7.10).

  Like before, dm ′ is just the effective non-dimensional frontal area of the ring at radius x. Without K ( x ), all the rings add up to a non-dimensional frontal area of 1, or ( 1 + ½ b ) for heavy loading. With K ( x ), the effective frontal area of the propeller will be reduced by a factor which we will calculate later.

17.4   The thrust reduction at optimal rotation

Equation (10.7) gave us the optimal axial induction distribution b(x) for a lightly loaded, rotationally optimal pro­peller of constant marginal loss C ( and constant overall loss ratio ½ C ) :

b ( x ) = C . cos 2 φ s (17.4)

Combining this with the mass flow (17.3) in the thrust equation (17.2) yields :

d T  ′ = C . cos 2 φ . K ( x ) . 2  x . d x X 2 (17.5)

The ring thrust is reduced by a factor of cos2 φ on the induction by (17.4), and by the factor K ( x ) on the mass flow by (17.3). Although the two reductions are very different in nature, both apply at the given efficiency.

  We define a notation for the combined thrust reduction factor :

G ( x ) ≡ cos 2 φ . K ( x ) (17.6)

The symbol G for this factor is a common convention in the literature. It is probably named after Sidney Goldstein, who made the first rigorous analysis of it in 1929.

  We will find that unfortunately, K ( x ) is not completely independent of the distribution of b ( x ). But clearly, the case for the optimal cos 2 φ distribution is of special interest.

17.5   The overall thrust reduction

In (11.3), we defined the symbol κφ for the integral of the local thrust reduction factor cos 2φ over the whole prop disk. We will define a similar factor κG for the combined effect (17.5) of this factor and the tip reduction K ( x ):

κ G  ( X ) ≡ 1 X 2  . ∫ 0 X cos 2  φ . K ( x )  . 2  x . d x (17.7)

17.6   Thrust reduction and efficiency

By direct comparison with chapter 11.2 before tip loss, we can write the same analysis with κG in the place of κφ :

T  ′ = C  .  κ G (17.8)
C = T  ′ κ G (17.9)
d P m  ′ d T  ′ = ½ . T  ′ κ G (17.10)

and finally :

η m = 1 1 + ½   .  T  ′ κ G (17.11)

The combined tip and rotational loss of (17.11) increases the momentum loss of the propeller by a factor of 1 / κG  relative to the ideal actuator disk efficiency (5.12). This loss is larger than that of (11.9) for rotation alone.
