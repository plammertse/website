INTRODUCTION

3   Thrust, power and efficiency

3.1   The thrust coefficient

This text will use the thrust from the propeller as the starting point of the design. We will use two definitions of the non-dimensional thrust coefficient, out of the many used in the literature. In analogy with the lift coefficient CL of a wing, we define the non-dimensional thrust coefficient CT for use outside propeller theory :

C T ≡ T ½ ρ V 2 . π R 2 (3.1)

This definition is based on ½V 2 instead of on the more usual ( ΩR )2. This reflects an emphasis in this text on the requirements of the aircraft, rather than on the torque or the RPM of the powerplant.

In propeller derivations it will be more convenient to divide by ρV 2 instead of by ½ ρV 2. We will denote this division by ρV 2 by a prime. This version of the non-dimensional thrust coefficient is then called T ' :

T  ′ ≡ T ρ V 2 . π R 2 (3.2)

It follows that :

T  ′ = C T (3.3)

Mechanical power has the dimension of thrust times velocity. We will therefore make power non-dimensional by dividing by V 3 instead of by V 2 :

P  ′ ≡ T ρ V 3 . π R 2 (3.4)

3.2   The net power

Mechanical power equals force times velocity. The net output power that a propeller exerts on an airplane is therefore the thrust multiplied by the flying speed :

P = T . V (3.5)

It is interesting to note that we can write (3.2) as a mechanical power as well as a force :

T  ′ = T . V ρ   V 2 . π  R 3 (3.6)

We can interpret T ′ as the non-dimensional thrust, but we can also interpret it as the net mechanical power T.V, made non-dimensional by the definition of (3.4).

3.3   Power loss and efficiency

Power equals force times velocity, but it also equals torque times rate of rotation ( with the rotation expressed in radians per second ), and energy per second.

Efficiency is defined as net power output divided by gross power input. Intuitively, loss of efficiency is associated with a loss of output for a given input. However, losses in a propeller are more easily calculated as an increase of the power input needed for a given power output. The efficiency is still the same ratio : output over input, but the losses are now written as fractional increases on the input, instead of fractional decreases on the output. The extra input still goes into energy lost to the wake of the propeller. This is why we keep calling these extra inputs "losses".

3.4   Loss ratios

The momentum loss and the viscous loss are increases on the input power ideally needed for a given output power T . V , and we will call these increases Pm and Pv . The propeller's efficiency then becomes :

η = T . V T . V + P m + P v (3.7)

We can divide top and bottom by the net power T.V :

η = 1 1 + P m T . V + P v T . V (3.8)

We will call the two fractions the momentum loss ratio and the viscous loss ratio. These power "losses" are really power increases, relative to the net power. They are non-dimensional, and they can be expressed in the non-dimensional thrust and power too. By (3.4) and (3.6) we have :

P T . V = P ′ T ′ (3.9)
η = 1 1 + P ′ m T ′ + P ′ v T ′ (3.10)

3.4   Types of loss in a propeller

There are four types of loss in a propeller :

-   axial momentum loss.

-   rotational momentum loss.

-   tip loss

-   viscous loss.

We will discuss each of these losses in due course. Each have their own distinct influence on the efficiency.

Momentum loss is the most basic and obvious one. Tip loss is not very different : it simply reduces the effective frontal area of the propeller, which increases the momentum loss for a given thrust. Viscous loss is different in nature. It is independent of the momentum loss, and has a very different relation to the pitch of the propeller.
