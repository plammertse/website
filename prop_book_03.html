3.2   The net power

Mechanical power equals force times velocity. The net output power that a propeller exerts on an airplane is therefore the thrust multiplied by the flying speed :

P = T . V (3.5)

It is interesting to note that we can write (3.2) as a mechanical power as well as a force :

T  ′ = T . V ρ   V 2 . π  R 3 (3.6)

We can interpret T ′ as the non-dimensional thrust, but we can also interpret it as the net mechanical power T.V made non-dimensional by the definition of (3.4).

3.4   Loss ratios

The momentum loss and the viscous loss are increases on the input power ideally needed for a given output power (T . V ), and we will call these increases Pm and Pv . The propeller's efficiency then becomes :

η = T . V T . V + P m + P v (3.7)

We can divide top and bottom by the net power T.V :

η = 1 1 + P m T . V + P v T . V (3.8)

We will call the two fractions the momentum loss ratio and the viscous loss ratio. These power "losses" are really power increases, relative to the net power. They are non-dimensional, and they can be expressed in the non-dimensional thrust and power too. By (3.4) and (3.6) we have :

P T . V = P ′ T ′ (3.9)
η = 1 1 + P ′ m T ′ + P ′ v T ′ (3.10)

3.4   Types of loss in a propeller

There are four types of loss in a propeller :

-   axial momentum loss.

-   rotational momentum loss.

-   tip loss

-   viscous loss.

We will discuss each of these losses in due course. Each have their own distinct influence on the efficiency.

Momentum loss is the most basic and obvious one. Tip loss is not very different : it simply reduces the effective frontal area of the propeller, which increases the momentum loss for a given thrust. Viscous loss is different in nature. It is independent of the momentum loss, and has a very different relation to the pitch of the propeller.
