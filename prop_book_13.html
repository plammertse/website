ROTATION

13   Blade chords ( before tip loss )

13.1   The blade chord for a given lift

Equation (11.8) gives the optimum thrust distribution including rotation. We will derive the corresponding blade chord. This chord does not yet include tip loss and viscous optimization. We will add those in later chapters.

  Equation (7.4) gave the thrust on a ring, and with the dm • of (7.8) for light loading, we have :

d T = d m • . V . b ( x ) = b ( x ) . ρ   V 2 . 2 π r . d r (13.1)

  The aerodynamic lift on the blade elements inside the ring must provide this thrust. The lift coefficient CL for a wing of area S is defined by :

L = C L . ½ ρ   V 2 .  S (13.2)

The blade area inside a ring is dS = B . c . dr, coming from B strips of width dr and chord c. The chord is defined in the direction of the local wind velocity W, as shown in the velocity diagram of figure 8.2. The lift generated by the total blade area dS within the ring is :

d L = C L .½ ρ   W 2 .  B .  c .  d r (13.3)

From figure 8.2, the local wind velocity W equals V / sin φ : x

d L = C L . ½  ρ .   V 2 sin 2 φ .  B .  c .  d r (13.4)

From the force diagram in figure 8.1, the thrust component of dL is :

d T = d L . cos φ (13.5)

We now have (13.1) for the ring thrust needed to generate an induction ratio b(x), and (13.5) for the ring thrust aerodynamically available from the blade elements to do this. The two must be equal :

ρ   . 2 π r . d r . b V 2 = C L . ½  ρ .   V 2 sin 2 φ .  B .  c .  d r . cos φ (13.6)

Dividing out the common factors ρ V 2 and dr, we have :

B . c 2π  r = 2 C L . b ( x ) . sin  φ . tan  φ (13.7)

The left hand term represents the fraction of the ring that is "covered by chord". We will call it the local solidity, but we have to note that the term "solidity" goes under many other definitions in the literature.

13.2   The non-dimensional solidity

Equation (2.10) defines x ≡ Ω r / V as the non-dimensional version of the radius r. We similarly define the non-dimensional chord c ′ :

c  ′ ≡ Ω   c V (13.8)

The ratio between c and r stays the same in the non-dimensional form. This will allow us to draw the blade planform (13.7) of c versus r in the same "true" proportions on a scale of c ′ versus x :

c r = c  ′ x   (13.9)

The non-dimensional local solidity stays the same as in (13.7) :

B . c  ′ 2π  x = 2 C L . b ( x ) . sin  φ . tan  φ (13.10)

With (2.4) and (2.5) we can express this completely in x :

B . c  ′ 2π  x = 2 C L . b ( x ) . 1 1 + x 2 . 1 x (13.11)

We will draw the blade shapes in proper proportion on the non-dimensional scale. With x on the horizontal axis, we have for the chord :

B . c  ′ = 4 π C L . b ( x ) . 1 1 + x 2
= 4 π C L . b ( x ) . sin  φ (13.12)

13.3   The blade shape for uniform loading

For uniform loading, (XXXX) gives us the constant induction b(x) = T ′. This turns (13.12) into :

B . c  ′ = 4 π C L . T  ′ .  sin  φ (13.13)

The non-dimensional blade shape is proportional to sin φ. We will call sin φ the chord shape factor fc,1 for uniform loading :

f c, 1 = sin  φ (13.14)

  Figure 13.1 shows this factor against x, as a red line.

  For larger values of x, this line approaches 1/x, since √ ( 1 + x2 ) tends to 1/x for larger x. Near the hub, φ ≅ 90° and sin φ ≅ 1. The non-dimensional chord factor becomes 1. Dividing the chord by 2 π x to get the "cover factor" on each ring shows that the local solidity will go to infinity near the hub. The propeller blades become layered like a Da Vinci helicopter, and in the end the chords wrap around the hub infinitely many times, for an infinitely small radius.

  Such shapes have actually been attempted in the past, but they are both impractical and unnecessary. In the rotationally optimal load distribution, the cos2 φ term comes to the rescue.

13.4   The blade shape for rotationally optimal loading

Rotational optimization will scale the thrust by cos2φ. With the light loading b(x) of (11.8), the non-dimensional chord (13.12) becomes :

  B .c^' = 4 π/C_L . T^'/κ_ ϕ . cos^2 ϕ . sin⁡ϕ (13.15)

With (2.7) for cos2φ, the blade shape factor becomes :

  f_(c,2) =〖 cos^2 ϕ . sin〗⁡〖ϕ = x^2/(1+x^2 ) . 1/√(1+x^2 )〗 (13.16)

Figure 13.1 shows this factor as a solid blue line.

Figure 13.1 :   Chord shapes for uniform and rotationally optimal distributions.

For large x, where ( 1 + x2 ) ≅ x2, the blade shape factor is approximately fc,2 ≅ 1/x just like in (13.14) for the uniform propeller.

  For small x we have ( 1+ x(sup>2) ≅ 1 and this yields fc,2 ≅ 1/x fc,2 ≅ x2. The local solidity (13.15) divides this by a factor of 1/x, but even then the result does not go to infinity. The local solidity in fact goes to zero at the hub, at a rate of 1/x. Unlike the uniformly loaded propeller, the rotationally optimal propeller has a viable blade shape. In practice, the chord will not go completely to zero near the hub, for mechanical reasons.

  The location of the widest chord is found by setting the derivative of (13.16) to zero. This yields tan2φ = ½, hence x = √2 :

  〖 x〗_ wide = √2 (13.17)

The chord factor at this point is :

  f_(c,2 max) = ⅔ . √(⅓) ≅ 0.385 (13.18)

Figure 13.1 indicates the widest point by a circle marker.

13.5   Inflating the chord to unit thrust

To normalize the thrust, we can make the factor 1/κφ in (13.15) part of the blade shape factor :

  f_(c,3) =〖1/κ_ ϕ . cos^2 ϕ . sin〗⁡ϕ (13.19)

Figure 13.2 shows the result. As for the thrust in figure 12.1, all rotationally optimal propellers are identical in shape, since the shape depends only on x. Blades of smaller X are just "cut off" sooner, at the desired tip speed ratio X.

13.6   The dimensional chord

Figures 13.1 and 13.2 showed the non-dimensional chord c &prinme; against x. The non-dimensional radius runs from 0 to X. This makes it hard to compare the shapes and the relative widths of various blades, since the blade planforms extend over different tip radius lengths X.

  We get a better impression of the shape variation if we plot all blades to a standard radius from 0 to 1. We achieve this by plotting c/R versus r/R. Relative to figure 13.2, we need to reduce both the horizontal and the vertical axis by 1/X. This changes (13.15) to :

  B . c/R = B . 〖 c〗^'/X = 4 π/C_L . T^'/κ_ ϕ . 〖1/X . cos〗^2 ϕ . sin⁡ϕ (13.20)

Figure 13.3 shows the result for two-bladed propellers of various tip speed ratios X. This figure is basically the same as figure 13.2, but this time with all blades scaled to a single radius. This new plot is dimensioned for our typical lightplane example of T ′ = 0.1 and CL = 0.4.

  By (13.17) the widest chord is located at r/R = √2/X, and by (13.18) the maximum width is :

  B . c_max/R = 4 π/C_L . T^'/κ_ ϕ . 0.385/X (13.21)

These points are marked by a circle marker. Apart from the secondary effect of 1/κφ , all else being equal, the width of the widest chord varies with 1/X.

13.7   The chord at the tip

At the tip, we have x = X. Expressing fc,2 in x like in (13.16), but substituting X for x, equation (13.20) now reads :

  B . c_tip/R = 4 π/C_L . T^'/κ_ ϕ . 1/X . X^2/(1+X^2 ) . 1/√(1+X^2 ) (13.22)

For larger X we have (1 + X 2 ) ≅ X 2, and we can approximate the tip chord by :

  B . c_tip/R ≅ 4 π/C_L . T^'/κ_ ϕ . 1/X^2 (13.23)

The tip chord is therefore roughly proportional to 1 / X 2.

13.8   The chord at 0.7 R

The tip is not always the most convenient place to measure the chord. The typical working radius for a propeller blade is r = 0.7 R, or x = 0.7 X.

  If the value of 0.7 X is fairly large relative to 1, then the same logic applies as for the tip chord, and we have :

  B . c_0.7/R ≅ 4 π/C_L . T^'/κ_ ϕ . 1/( 0.7 X^2 ) (13.24)

The chord at 70 % of the radius is roughly proportional to 1 / X 2. Higher pitch propellers therefore have much wider blades, as was already obvious from figure 13.3.

13.9   Blade taper

With (13.21) the widest chord at x = √2 is proportional to 1 / X, and by (13.23) and (13.24) the chords farther out are proportional to 1 / X 2. Figure 13.3 shows how this works out in practice. Propellers of high pitch ( hence of low X ) have paddle-like shapes, with the tips nearly as wide as the widest point at x = √2. Propellers of high X are clearly tapered. Chapter XXXXXXX will show that this result holds even after tip loss.

  The reason for the chord variation is the variation in the local airspeed on the blade elements. The velocity diagram of figure 2.3 shows that for larger values of x ≡ Ω r / V, the local wind speed W is very similar to Ω r. This makes W proportional to x, with W ≅ x . V. The lift on a blade element is proportional to the local airspeed squared. All else being equal, this makes the lift per chord width locally proportional to x2, and at the tip to X2. This ratio is reflected in the tip chord width of (13.23).
