Propellers

Propellers have caught my attention early on. Below is a short list of applications that further sparked my interest. The tabs above give access to my take on propeller theory.

Rubber powered models

Ever since I tried to fly rubber powered models as a kid, I have had a keen interest in propellers.

In Delft, I followed the mesmerizing lectures by prof. Dobbinga on the subject. In retrospect, they were good Dutch translations and re-interprations of Glauert's classic treatment in Durand. But in the Delft curriculum it was not the habit to let students use the original literature.

I tried to apply Dobbinga's theory to my models, but frankly they never performed the way I hoped.

Human powered flight

Much later, I found articles by Eugene Larrabee on the vast increase in performance of a paddle-shaped pro­pel­ler over straight blades in the "Gossamer Condor" man-powered aircraft. It made the difference between a flight of 1 mile maximum and a flight from Crete to Santorini.

Computer programs

I tried my hand at implementing classical propeller design in what turned out to be Adkins' version of Larrabee's version of Glauert's version of Betz's method as given in Durand, first on Texas TI-58 calculators, then on a Sharp MZ-700 MSX home computer, and finally on PC's in Matlab. There it stopped for a while.

Electric flight

Recently, my interest was rekindled by a project at InHolland University in Delft to electrify a Dragonfly canard aircraft. In the end this led to writing a small book on propeller theory, and this in turn led to revisiting the idea of optimizing the viscous loss along with the other losses which Glauert had solved only partly, and which I believe has since been mostly ignored.
