ROTATION

15   The spiral wake

15.1   Introduction

A large part of propeller theory, including the tip loss, is concerned with the conditions inside the wake.

  In a turn of good fortune, the rotationally optimal induction distribution (10.7) simplifies the wake sheets to a regular Archimedes screw. We will see later that this shape is not disturbed by the tip loss, and in fact can be used to find a very useful approximation for that loss.

15.2   The rotating, telescoping wake

The induction velocity vector v ′ in figure 8.2 has an axial component v, and a circumferential ( tangential, sideways ) component u. The axial reduction equals cos 2 φ. From standard trigonometry, we then have :

v = cos 2 φ = ½ + ½  cos  2 φ (15.1)

From the same figure, the tangential component u equals tan φ times the axial component v :

u = cos 2 φ . tan φ = sin φ . cos φ (15.2)

From standard trigonometry we have :

u = ½  sin  2 φ (15.3)

The axial and tangential components can thus both be expressed as functions of the "double pitch angle" 2 φ.

  Figure 15.1 shows that by these two equations, the ends of the induced velocity vector v ′ lie on a circle with radius ½ C and “phase” 2 φ.

Figure 15.1 :   The optimal induction as a function of the local pitch angle.

  Far from the hub, the local pitch angle φ is nearly zero. The induced velocity vector v ′ points almost straight aft ( down in the picture ), and its length is nearly C. Very close to the hub, φ ≅ 90°. Here v ′ is nearly tangential, and its length is almost zero. At the radius x = 1, the vector points 45° down and has the length ½ 2  C.

  We can see from this picture that the flow tube must be filled with hollow, telescoping tubes of air, which both rotate and slide relative to each other. The inconvenient truth that these telescoping tubes must "rub" against each other is one reason why the independence of the rings is not an obvious fact, but a non-obvious assump­tion, in the theory of the optimal propeller.
