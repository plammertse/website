CHORDS

27   Blade chords

27.1   A first dimensional analysis

This chapter will derive the blade chords needed for a given thrust ( or induction ).

  A first dimensional idea can be obtained by looking at the case of small pitch angles.

  A ring of the prop disk is "served" by the area of the blade elements in the ring. The thrust on these blade ele­ments must provide the induction in the whole of the ring. From figure 8.2, the circum­ferential airspeed of the blade at small pitch angles is W ≅ Ω.r. The lift of the blade elements is proportional to ½ ρ W 2, and therefore the blade lift is proportional to r 2.

  The blade elements at that radius cover a ring of area 2πr. Comparing the two, we can expect the chord needed for a given induction to be proportional to 1 / r. This is the basic shape of a propeller blade. We will see below that it is modified by rotational optimization and tip loss, and of course by the larger pitch angles near the hub, but in principle, the blade outline will have a hyperbolic taper.

  A less obvious consequence is that propellers of high tip speed ratio X will have narrower chords than propel­lers of low X, in the ratio 1 / X. High pitch propellers have much wider blades than propellers of lower pitch. This will become clear after studying the scaling difference between the non-dimensional and the dimensional chord.

27.1   The blade chord for a given local induction

Equation ( 7.5 ) gave us the thrust for a given induction and mass flow, and with the light loading mass flow of ( 7.7 ), we obtain the following ring thrust :

d T = d m • . V . b ( x ) = b ( x ) . ρ   V 2 . 2 π  r . d r (27.1)

The aerodynamic lift on the blade elements inside the ring must provide this thrust. The lift coefficient CL for a wing of area S is defined by :

L = C L . ½ ρ   V 2 .  S (27.2)

The blade area inside a ring is dS = B . c . dr, coming from B blade element strips of width dr and chord c. The chord is defined in the direction of the local wind velocity W.

  The lift generated by the total blade element area ( all the chord strips ) within the ring is :

d L = C L .½ ρ   W 2 .  B .  c .  d r (27.3)

From figure 8.2, the local wind velocity W equals V / sin φ :

d L = C L . ½  ρ .   V 2 sin 2 φ .  B .  c .  d r (27.4)

From the force diagram in figure 8.1, the thrust component of dL is :

d T = d L . cos φ (27.5)

We now have (27.1) for the ring thrust needed to generate the induction ratio b (x), and (27.5) for the ring thrust available from blade element lift to do this. The two must be equal :

ρ   . 2 π r . d r . b V 2 =   C L . ½  ρ .   V 2 sin 2 φ .  B .  c .  d r . cos φ (27.6)

Dividing out the common factors ρ V 2 and dr we have :

B . c 2π  r = 2 C L . b ( x ) . sin  2 φ cos  φ (27.7)

The left hand term represents the fraction of the ring that is "covered by chord". We will call it the local solidity, but we do note that the term "solidity" goes under many different definitions in the literature.

27.2   The non-dimensional solidity

We will first derive a non-dimensional chord on the same scale as the non-dimensional radius x. This will allow us to draw the blade in the proper proportions.

  With span and width scaled to the same non-dimen­sional units, each blade will have a "span" of X. Blades of larger X will have a larger span on the horizontal axis. The blades will vary in length, but hardly in width.

  We will then rescale the blades to have equal radius. This will scale down the chords with X. These plots will give a different perspective on the variation of the chord with the nominal propeller pitch, or tip speed ratio.

  Equation (2.10) defines x ≡ Ω r / V as the non-dimensional version of the radius r. We similarly define the non-dimensional chord c ′ :

c  ′ ≡ Ω   c V (27.8)

The ratio between c and r stays the same in the non-dimensional form. This will allow us to draw the blade planform (27.7) of c versus r in the same "true" proportions on a scale of c ′ versus x :

c r = c  ′ x   (27.9)

From (27.7), the non-dimensional local solidity becomes :

B . c  ′ 2π  x = 2 C L . b ( x ) . sin  2 φ cos  φ (27.10)

With sin φ / cos φ = tan φ = 1 / x from ( 2.2 ), we can eliminate x. Bringing the factor of 2π to the right we have :

B . c  ′ = 4 π C L . b ( x ) . sin  φ (27.11)

27.3   The blade shape for uniform loading

For uniform loading, ( 5.10 ) gives us the constant induction b (x) = T ′. This turns (27.11) into :

B . c  ′ = 4 π C L . T  ′ .  sin  φ (27.12)

The non-dimensional blade shape for uniform loading ( i.e. without rotational optimization ) is proportional to sin φ. Figure 27.1 shows this factor against x, as a red line.

Figure 27.1 :   Non-dimensional shape factors for uniform
       and rotationally optimal distributions.

For larger values of x, we have small local pitch angles φ, where φ in φ ≅ tan φ ≅ 1 / x, and so :

B . c  ′ ≅ 4 π C L . T  ′  .  1 x x  ≫  1 (27.13)

The figure shows this as a grey line.

  Near the hub, φ ≅ 90° and sin φ ≅ 1. The non-dimensional chord factor becomes 1. Dividing the chord factor by the local ( non-dimensional ) circumference 2 π. x shows that the local solidity will go to infinity near the hub.

  The propeller blades become layered like a Da Vinci helicopter, or a whipped cream swirl, with the chords wrapping around the hub infinitely many times on an infinitely small radius. Such shapes have actually been attempted in the past, but they are both impractical and unnecessary.

  We will see that in the rotationally optimal load distribution, the cos2 φ term comes to the rescue.

27.4   The blade shape for rotationally optimal loading

Rotational optimization will scale the thrust by cos2 φ. With the light loading b (x) of ( 11.10 ), the non-dimensional chord (27.11) becomes :

B . c′ = 4π CL . T′ κ φ . cos 2 φ . sin  φ (27.14)

Figure 27.1 shows this factor as a solid blue line. For large x, where cos 2 φ ≅ 1, the blade shape factor is app­rox­imately ≅ 1 / x , just like with the sin φ for the uniform propeller.

  For small x, by ( 2.5 ) and ( 2.7 ) the shape factor becomes approximately x2. For the local solidity we have to divide by 2π x, so the solidity goes to zero at the hub with x. In practice, the chord will not go completely to zero, if only for mechanical reasons. Unlike the uniformly loaded propeller, the local chord and solidity do not go to infinity at the hub. The rotationally optimal propeller has a viable blade shape.

  The location of the widest chord is found by setting the derivative of cos2 φ . sin φ to zero. This yields
cos3 φ − 2 . cos φ . sin2 φ = 0, hence tan2 φ = ½, hence x = 1 / tan φ = √2 :

x wide = √2 (27.15)

Plotting a little triangle with sides of 1, √2 and √3, the chord factor at this point is :

cos 2 φ . sin  φ = ⅔ . √⅓ = 0.385 (27.16)

Figure 27.1 indicates the widest point by a circle marker.

27.5   Inflating the chord to unit thrust

To normalize the thrust, we can make the factor 1 / κφ in (27.14) part of the blade shape factor.

  Figure 27.2 shows the result. Still like in figure 27.1, all rotationally optimal propellers are identical in shape. The shape factor depends only on x. Blades of smaller X are just "cut off" sooner.

Figure 27.2 :   Rotationally optimal chord for unit thrust.

27.6   The dimensional chord

Figures 27.1 and 27.2 showed the non-dimensional chord c ′ against x.

  The non-dimensional radius runs from 0 to X. This makes it hard to compare the shapes and the relative widths of various blades, since the blade planforms extend over different tip radius lengths X.

  We get a better impression of the shape variation if we plot all blades to a standard radius from 0 to 1. We achieve this by plotting c / R versus r / R. Relative to figure 27.2, we need to reduce both the horizontal and the vertical axis by 1/X. With (27.9) we have c / R = c′ / X. This changes (27.14) to :

B . c R = B . c ′ X = 4π CL . T′ κ φ . 1> X . cos 2 φ . sin  φ (27.17)

Figure 27.3 shows the result for two-bladed propellers of various tip speed ratios X. This figure is basically the same as figure 27.2, but this time with all blades scaled to a single radius. This new plot is dimensioned for our typical lightplane example of T ′ = 0.1 and CL = 0.4.

27.7   The widest chord

By (27.15) the widest chord is located at r / R = √2 / X, and by (27.16) the maximum width is :

B . c max R = 4π CL . T′ κ φ . 0.385 X (27.18)

These points are marked by a circle marker. Apart from the secondary effect of 1 / κφ , all else being equal, the width of the widest chord varies with 1 / X.

Figure 27.3 :   Rotationally optimal chords for varying X.
       Two blades, thrust T ' = 0.1, CL = 0.4.

27.7   The chord at the tip

At the tip, we have x = X. equation (27.18) now reads :

B . c tip R = 4π CL . T′ κ φ . 1> X . X 2 1 + X 2 . 1 1 + X 2 (27.19)

For larger X we have ( 1 + X 2 ) ≅ X2, and we can approximate the tip chord by :

B . c tip R = 4π CL . T′ κ φ . 1> X 2 (27.20)

The tip chord is therefore roughly proportional to 1 / X 2.

27.8   The chord at 0.7 R

Because in practice the tip is rounded, it is not always the most convenient place to measure the chord. The typical working radius for a propeller blade is r = 0.7 R, or x = 0.7 X.

  If the value of 0.7 X is fairly large relative to 1, then the same logic applies as for the tip chord, and we have :

B . c 0.7R R = 4π CL . T′ κ φ . 1> 0.7  X 2 (27.21)

The chord at 70 % of the radius is roughly proportional to 1 / X 2. Higher pitch propellers therefore have much wider blades, as was already obvious from figure 27.3.

27.9   Blade taper

With (27.23), the widest chord at x = √2 is proportional to 1 / X.

  By (27.23) and (27.24) the chords farther out are proportional to 1 / X 2. Figure 27.3 shows how this works out in practice.
In shape, these propellers are all identical. A propeller of X = √2 would have its widest point at the tip. A propeller of i>X = 2.5 has its widest point nearly halfway. Propellers of higher X start to resemble the 1 / X shape. Propellers of high pitch ( hence of low X ) have paddle-like shapes, with the tips nearly as wide as the widest point at x = √2.

Propellers of high X are clearly tapered. Chapter XXXXXXX will show that this result holds even after tip loss.

  The reason for the chord variation is the variation in the local airspeed on the blade elements. The velocity diagram of figure 2.3 shows that for larger values of x ≡ Ω r / V, the local wind speed W is very similar to Ω r. This makes W proportional to x, with W ≅ x . V. The lift on a blade element is proportional to the local airspeed squared. All else being equal, this makes the lift per chord width locally proportional to x2, and at the tip to X2. This ratio is reflected in the tip chord width of (27.23).
