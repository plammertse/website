AXIAL MOMENTUM

5   Thrust and the mass flow

5.1   The mass flow at light loading

So far, the mass flow dropped out of our equations for the efficiency. The efficiencies (4.14) and (4.18) depended only on the induction a, c.q. b. By Newton’s law (4.7) however, repeated here as (5.1), the thrust does depend on the mass flow :

T = m • . w = m • . b . V (5.1)

By this route, the mass flow will indirectly affect the efficiency for a given thrust after all.

  If the mass flow m● is somehow reduced, and we will find later that this is the case in the tip loss, then for a given thrust T the induction w will have to be increased. By (4.18), this increase in w will cause a loss of effic­iency. We will loosely come to call this loss of efficiency "the tip loss", but it is really a loss of thrust for a given efficiency.

  We will first look at the mass flow, before tip loss, for the ideal actuator disk of uniform induction. In the ideal actuator disk of figure 4.1, the volume of air passing through the prop disk per second equals the disk frontal area π R 2 times the velocity (  V + v ). The mass flow m● equals this volume, times the density ρ ( in kg/m3  )  of the passing air :

m • =   ρ  . π  R 2 .  (  V + v  )
= ρ . V . π  R 2 .  (  1 + a  ) (5.2)

This mass flow is slightly higher than the mass flow without an induction a, and by (5.1), this will give slightly more thrust. Since this is a secondary effect, and it complicates many of the later derivations, we will often use the so-called "light loading" approximation. This assumes v ≪ V, or a ≪ 1, or n other words ( V+ v ) ≅ V.

This simplifies (5.2) for the lightly loaded ideal actuator disk into :

m • = ρ . V . π  R 2 (5.3)

5.2   The non-dimensional mass flow m '

In later chapters on the tip loss, it will be useful to have a non-dimensional version of the mass flow since tip loss acts as a reduction factor on it. Using the ideal mass flow of (5.3) as a reference, we define :

m  ′ ≡ m • ρ . V . π  R 2 (5.4)

This means that for the lightly loaded, ideal actuator disk we have :

m  ′ = 1 (5.5)

The fact that a physical propeller can even approach the ideal of accelerating all of the air inside the flow tube and none outside it like in figure 4.1, and that it therefore can approach m ′  = 1 ( before tip loss ) is by non means obvious. We will find a justification from the vortex view later. For the pur­poses of this chapter and the next ones, we will just accept it as a given.

5.3   The non-dimensional effective disk area

If we do not follow the light loading assumption, then by applying the definition (5.4) to (5.2) we find :

m  ′ = ( 1 + a  ) (5.6)

The heavily loaded propeller handles slightly more mass than the lightly loaded one of (5.3). We can also interpret this as a slight increase in the effective frontal disk area which captures the incoming air. We derived the increase in mass flow by inspection of figure 4.1, from the speed increase at the prop disk.

  But from the same figure we might say that the propeller handles air flowing in from far ahead, at the un­accel­erated velocity V, through a larger frontal area of the flow tube before contraction.

  The frontal area of this incoming air is larger than the prop disk by a factor of ( 1+a ). We can therefore also interpret m ′ as an effective ( non-dimensional ) disk area slightly larger than one.

5.4   The non-dimensional thrust T '

We can make the thrust non-dimensional by applying (3.2) to (5.1) :

T = m • . b . V ρ   V 2 . π  R 2 (5.7)

By a canceling of terms, the non-dimensional mass flow of (5.4) changes this to :

T  ′ = m  ′ .  b (5.9)

For light loading we can use the m ′ = 1 of (5.5). This yields :

T  ′ = b (5.10)

5.5   The ideal efficiency expressed in T '

Equation (4.18) gave us the efficiency of an ideal, lightly loaded actuator disk in terms of the far wake axial induction b. For light loading, we can change this into a relation with the thrust coefficient by (5.10) and (3.3) :

η m = 1 1 + ½  T  ′ = 1 1 + ¼  C T (5.12)

The momentum loss ratio (4.17) is :

P m  ′ T ′ = ½  T  ′ = ¼  C T (5.13)
