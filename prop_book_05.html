AXIAL MOMENTUM

5   Light loading thrust and mass flow

5.1   The mass flow at light loading

So far, the mass flow dropped out of our equations for the efficiency. The efficiencies (4.12) and (4.16) depend only on the induction a, c.q. b. We will find that even though the mass flow may not affect the efficiency, it does affect the thrust, so we look at it now.

  In the ideal actuator disk of figure 4.1, the volume of air passing through the prop disk per second, equals the disk frontal area π R 2 times the velocity (  V + v ). The mass flow equals this volume times its density ρ ( in kg/m3  ) :

m • =   ρ  . π  R 2 .  (  V + v  )
= ρ . V . π  R 2 .  (  1 + a  ) (5.1)

  However, we will often use the so-called "light loading" approximation, which assumes that v ≪ V, or a ≪ 1, or in other words : ( V+ v ) ≅ V. This simplifies (5.1) to :

m • = ρ . V . π  R 2 (5.2)

  The fact that a physical propeller can approach the ideal of accelerating all of the air inside the flow tube and none outside it, is by non means obvious. We will find a justification from the vortex view later. For the purposes of this chapter and the next, we will accept it as a given.

5.2   The non-dimensional mass flow

In later chapters on the tip loss it will be useful to have a non-dimensional version of the mass flow, since tip loss acts as a reduction factor on it. Using the ideal mass flow of (5.3) as a reference, we define :

m  ′ ≡ m • ρ   V . π  R 2 (5.4)

This means that for the ideal propeller, we have :

m  ′ = 1 (5.5)

5.3   The effective disk area

If we do not follow the light loading assumption, then by applying the definition (5.4) to (5.2) we find :

m  ′ = ( 1 + a  ) (5.6)

The heavily loaded propeller handles slightly more mass than that of (5.3).

For (5.2) we derived this increase in mass flow from the speed increase at the prop disk. But from the same figure we might say that the propeller handles the air flowing in from far ahead at the unaccelerated velocity V through a larger frontal area of the flow tube, before contraction. The frontal area of this incoming air is larger than the prop disk by a factor of ( 1+a ). We can therefore also interpret m ′ as an effective disk area ratio, slightly larger than one.

5.4   Non-dimensional thrust

We can make the thrust (3.2) non-dimensional by applying (4.7) to (3.2) :

T = m •  w ρ   V 2 . π  R 2 (5.7)

With b = w / V from (4.2) this is :

T  ′ = m •  V ρ   V 2 . π  R 2  .  b (5.8)

The non-dimensional mass flow of (5.4) changes this to :

T  ′ = m  ′ .  b (5.9)

For light loading we can use the unity m ′ of (5.5) :

T  ′ = b (5.10)

If we drop the light loading assumption we have :

T  ′ = b . ( 1 + a  ) = b . ( 1 + ½  b  ) (5.11)

These are the values for uniform induction b and a fully filled flow tube π R2. We will see later that b may vary over the prop disk, for instance for rotational optimization, and m ′ may not fill the whole flow tube, due to tip loss.

5.5   The ideal efficiency expressed in T '

Equation (4.18) gave us the efficiency of an ideal, lightly loaded actuator disk in terms of the far wake axial induction b. For light loading, we can change this into a relation with the thrust coefficient by (5.10) and (3.3) :

η m = 1 1 + ½  T  ′ = 1 1 + ¼  C T (5.12)

The momentum loss ratio (4.17) is :

P m  ′ T ′ = ½  T  ′ = ¼  C T (5.13)

5.6   The T ' for heavy loading

Airplane propellers have relatively small induction ratios in cruising flight, typically on the order of b = 0.1 like in our running numerical example. Our equations, starting with (5.11) and followed by many, will stay much simpler if we assume ½ b ≪ 1 and therefore ( 1 + a ) ≅ 1. It is common to develop the theory under this so-called "light loading assumption".

We briefly touch on the general case of "heavy" loading. We can solve (5.11) for b by writing it as a quadratic :

b 2 + 2  b - 2  T  ′ = 0 (5.14)

Solving the quadratic gives one positive solution :

b = 1 + 2 T  ′ - 1 (5.15)

Figure 5.1 shows this relation. For a given induction ratio and efficiency, the contraction of the flow tube draws more air through the prop disk. This increases the thrust at the same efficiency, which gives the propeller a slight advantage. If desired, we can correct our calculations for the contraction by iteratively adapting the radius R of the propeller to a slightly larger value, by a factor of ( 1 + ½ b ).

Strictly, we should carry the ( 1 + ½ b ) term right through all of our calculations, including the derivatives in the rotational optimization. This is not hard to do, but the small increase in accuracy for heavy loading does not outweigh the added complexity of the equations obscuring our intuitive understanding.

We will stay with the light loading assumption, mainly because it keeps the central arguments easier to follow.
