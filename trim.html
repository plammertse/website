Trim

trim and stability

Trim is the equilibrium condition where the total pitching moment of the airplane is zero. This is a necessary condition for steady flight, but it is not enough.

  To be useful, the trimmed condition also needs to be stable. We will discuss this elsewhere. Briefly, the air­plane needs to act like a weathervane, with the center of its "side area" aft of the pivot. In free flight, the CG is the pivot. The center of the side area is called the AC ("aerodynamic center").

ways of seeing

Trim is often treated as part of the stability analysis of the airplane, and that analysis is definitely needed first.

  But I believe it can be instructive to take that part for granted, and then look at trim again from a more static point of view. This can cast a useful light on tail loads and trim drag, and even on the design differences between canard and conventional aircraft.

two independent terms in the tailplane load

It is a bit unusual, but very enlightening to separate out two independent functions of the tailplane ( or canard ).

  One function is to cancel the so-called zero-lift moment of the airframe. This is an aerodynamic coefficient, which comes mostly comes from the curvature of the wing section, and perhaps the fuselage.

  Compensating for this aerodynamic coefficient requires nothing more than a fixed offset to the lift coefficient and therefore to the angle of attack, of the tailplane or canard.

  Since all aerodynamic forces increase with the square of the airspeed, its contribution to the tailplane force starts from zero at zero velocity, and grows quadratically with the flying speed. But apart from the physical force, once set, this offset can be forgotten. With the tailplane set at this fixed angle, the airplane has zero lift and zero moment at all airspeeds. It is, if you like, trimmed for zero lift.

  The second component of the tailplane lift is to change the trim from zero lift to some other lift value.

zero moment at zero lift

A normal, downward curved wing section gives a nose down moment at zero lift. To trim it, even for zero lift, we need to add a positive ( nose up ) moment. The positive moment at zero lift is normally provided by a stabilizer pushing down or a canard pushing up, at zero lift. This is accomplished by a difference in angle of attack, or rather incidence setting between the wing and the stabilizer, which is officially called "decalage", with a nice 1914 vintage French word. The difference needed is typically something like 3°. To keep zero lift, the wing needs a little bit of up lift ( hence angle of attack( ), to compensate for the down lift on the stabilizer, but since the tail arm to the CG is much longer than the wing arm, to a first approximation the moment equation is ( leaving out the common term of ½ ρ V 2 ) :

  Cm ac . S . c = Δ CL,h . Sh . lh .

zero moment at positive lift

We now add a net lift, but we keep a zero moment about the CG. Figure 1 gives the situation.

  The lift is provided by the wing lift Lh and the stabilizer lift Lh. If the wing AC is a distance xCG ahead of the CG and the stabilizer AC is at a distance of lh behind it, then the moment equation is :

  Lw . xCG = Δ Lh . lh 

This equation does not change with speed or trim, so the extra lift on the stabilizer on top of what is needed to counter the wing's Cm ac always has the same ratio to the lift of the wing. Unlike the basic decalage, this ratio does not change with airspeed. What does change strongly with airspeed are the non-dimensional values of CLw and CLh, but always in that same ratio.
